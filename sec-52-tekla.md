*(This post is in reference to the Indian Copyright Act, 2012, and the problem of TPMs/EULA based restrictions on users' software freedoms. This is an isolated fragment and doesn't
constitute the bulk of my thoughts on the matter. It is also only conjecture, not legal advice. By "EULA", I mean the shrinkwrap contract users typically agree to that block
them from exercising any of the 4 freedoms of software)*

Tekla Corporation vs Survo Ghosh[1]'s opinion seems to unambigously state EULAs can't restrict Sec.52 rights, and further implies TPMs aren't allowed to restrict them either. Reading Tekla Para 17, I understand reverse engineering/interoperability EULA restrictions are illegal, and anticonsumer/anticompetitive TPMs are very legally suspect?

Para 17 of Tekla:

> I am also of the opinion that even if the holder of a copyright, though not entitled in law to impose any restrictions curtailing the fair use thereof or in the nature of curbing creativity (and which is against public policy), still contractually or otherwise imposes such restrictions or prohibits doing of anything permitted by Section 52 or any other provision of the Copyright Act or any other law, the same would not bind anyone or be enforceable in law and thereby not effect anyone."

contractually" can refer to EULAs, while "or otherwise" could imply TPMs or technical lockouts/bans.

AFAIK, this was never brought up or referred to in this case (Aarogya Setu), Engineering Analysis Centre of Excellence Pvt Ltd. vs CIT[2], or any other case, not even in any public/academic discussion. I wonder if I'm missing something obvious. Tekla's opinion, to me, makes this far more defined/biased of a gap, and I'm extremely interested in seeing it clarified.

The effects of clearing this gap up would be significant for user rights and software competition. If Tekla's opinion gets proactively upheld, users could make and use third-party clients, mods, plugins, server federation, etc without legal threats or technical retaliation (e.g account bans, witholding service/data, integrity/tamper checking), and software rightsholders would have no basis to legally or technically restrict them (and users could have a legal recourse against technical retaliation against them for modding?) It would effectively be a right for users to escape/violate technological walled gardens.

The current status quo in this gap is that EULAs have carte blanche to legally restrict Sec.52 rights, and software rightsholders are allowed to technically restrict or scare you out of exercising Sec.52 rights (e.g account bans, witholding service or data access, proactively developing more tamper detection/banning). Most harms in software come down to users' (technical or legal) inability to use or modify it so it suits their interests and doesn't exploit/spy on them.

# My bitter conclusion

I don't like to admit this because it means then I would also be guilty of the exact laziness and fear.

But the reality is somebody has to **sue.** Somebody in India has to, for example, write a custom Instagram client, then when they get banned and Cease-and-desisted by Meta, **sue them in court** for Meta's action, citing Section 52 and its' design, maybe with some consumer rights and anticompetition angle thrown in too for good measure. This person would need a deep grip on the law/precedent/legislative intention on his side, as well as international arguments from the EFF, FSF, etc. His lawyers would need to be good too, well versed in the arguments and uncompromising. 

The case might drag on for 10 years and cost 100 crores or something, take up all his time, burn him out 3-4 times, and he'd have to keep at it anyway, uncompromising in the belief in fair dealing. Not to mention he might get absolutely slimed out and banned by every other mainstream platform, e.g Google, Microsoft, Apple, or Meta.

Ultimately the power relation between the company and the user (or even userbase) is determined precisely by the fact that the company prevents its' userbase from leaving it, competing with it, interoperating with it, or seeking alternatives, through TPM. This asymmetry of power must be taken into account as a pro-competition and consumer-rights based justificatory point for justifying our side of things to the court.

Ultimately if it's true that Section 52 rights cannot be waived away by contract in view of public policy, the ultimate aim of copyright of public creativity, etc. then every single EULA clause containing shit like this would be null and void in India:
